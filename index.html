Bukas, how it works

You write something and hold the button to seal it. Bukas keeps it and picks a day to give it back. That could be in two weeks or in four years. You don't get to choose, and you won't know when.

Until then you can't open it, search for it or read it again. There's no archive and nothing to scroll through. You just wait, and one day it turns up.

It's a place to write for no one, not even the person you'll be later. Nothing gets counted and nothing nags you to come back. When something does return, read it, and then decide if you want to seal it again.

Bukas is Tagalog for tomorrow. It also means open.

How it works

You write something, hold to seal it, and it’s gone. You can’t reread it, search it or browse it. At some point it comes back. Could be days, could be years, and you don’t get to pick. Sometimes two turn up together from different years with nothing to explain why. Once you’ve read it, you can seal it again, put it off, or let it go for good.

Why it works that way

Every other journalling app is built to help you get more out of what you wrote. Search, prompts, summaries, mood graphs, an AI that explains you to yourself. All of it assumes the problem is that you haven’t had enough insight yet. Usually that isn’t the problem. When you can go back whenever you want, you go back when you’re already in a certain mood, and you end up finding the entries that match it. Bukas takes that choice off you, so your own words turn up when you weren’t expecting them. That’s the only way they’re going to read differently.

There’s nothing here to finish. No streaks, no counts, no badges, nothing telling you how much is sealed. Nothing happens if you stop. Nothing gets measured either, because once something is measured it turns into a thing you’re supposed to improve, and that isn’t what this is.

Your writing stays yours

You can export everything whenever you want, sealed entries included. The lock is part of the app, not the file. Your entries sit against your account so they can find you again years from now, and nothing reads what’s inside them. The system only ever looks at dates.

What it’s built on

Albert Camus wrote about the absurd, the gap between wanting life to make sense and a world that doesn’t give you an answer. He didn’t think the goal was to close that gap. If you rush to explain it away, you stop really looking at your life. Bukas doesn’t try to explain anything to you either. An entry just comes back, and what it means is up to you.

“One must imagine Sisyphus happy.” Albert Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus

Jean-Paul Sartre argued that you’re responsible for your life even when you didn’t choose how it started or what happens to you. You can’t pick when your writing returns, but you can pick what you do with it when it does. That small choice is the part that belongs to you.

“Man is condemned to be free.” Jean-Paul Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism

Byung-Chul Han describes how a lot of pressure today doesn’t come from a boss or anyone else. We put it on ourselves, always trying to be more productive and a better version of ourselves, until even rest feels like something to get right. That’s why nothing here is counted. There’s no streak to protect and no score to improve, so writing can just be writing.

“The achievement-subject exploits itself until it burns out.” Byung-Chul Han, The Burnout Society

John Vervaeke calls what a lot of people are feeling the meaning crisis. We have more information than ever, but less sense of what actually matters. His point is that more facts about yourself won’t fix that. What helps is seeing things from a different angle. Reading something you wrote months or years ago, when you weren’t expecting it, is one small way of doing that. You meet your own words as a slightly different person.

“Ignorance is lack of knowledge. Foolishness is a lack of wisdom.” John Vervaeke, Awakening from the Meaning Crisis
